Abstract

            The healthcare industry is evolving its practices around data to be more cost effective, bring better care to more people, and improve worker productivity. This has been spurred by the mass adoption of mobile computing, as well as U.S. regulations demanding more readily auditable databases to ensure patient data privacy. However, the chaotic and decentralized nature of U.S. healthcare means that progress is uneven and slow; healthcare has always been half a decade behind other industries in adopting new technologies, and still struggles with legacy systems born in the days of paper records. This paper summarizes a project about understanding these problems through examining case studies creating custom dashboards as examples of data driven design meant to solve the problems faced by patients and practitioners alike.

Motivations for Better Health Information Systems

            Modern Electronic Health Records (EHRs) have their roots in the paper-based processes of decades past.  HIPAA becoming law, the September 11 attack, the Anthrax scare, and the proliferation and miniaturization of computers created a cascade of change in the healthcare industry. There was a need for data to become more secure, more searchable, more able to be audited. At the same time, an opportunity presented itself, to take data previously too difficult to study and transform it into a database where analysis is easily performed and push the entire healthcare field into a more data-driven direction.

Many of the case studies in Healthcare Information Management Systems: Cases, Studies, and Solutions (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016) express how much friction is present between the analysts and the practitioners. Data analysis is best done in code, creating custom views to understand very specific patterns in the data. They work at a desk, and much of their work will be seen by their eyes only, and only translated for outside viewing toward the end of their process. This is the most traditional way data work has been done, and even to this day, much of the software used for analytics assumes this traditional setting.

Healthcare practitioners buck this trend, having to balance time reading data with their regular duties to patients. They do not have the luxury to code, nor to sit down; they are responsible for all patients that come through their doors, and that does not leave them with enough time to conduct ad hoc analysis. This is the central friction: the practitioner is best served by careful data analysis that adheres to their existing workflow, a workflow that is chaotic at the best of times.

This dilemma gives rise to the need for new EHRs that give nurses and doctors relevant patient data in a form that is quick to understand, while being comprehensive enough to not miss important notes. Case Studies 1, 3, and 9 detail this challenge: the importance of designing software with the end user in mind from the beginning of any software project. For example, baking features into the EHR to accommodate a chaotic workflow. A nurse can see many patients in a single day, making them very sensitive to how the EHR alerts them to important information. “Alert Fatigue” is a phenomenon where someone will begin to ignore those alerts if they appear too frequently, which risks missing important information like a drug allergy. Therefore, the software’s design needs to readily understand the relative importance of each piece of information from the patient’s medical record and treat them differently so as not to “burn out” the practitioner (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016).

This challenge is worth attempting! The ideal, future versions of EHRs will allow nurses and doctors alike to spend the minimal amount of time inputting data and navigating software, and more time being attentive to patients, reduce wait times, and bring better health outcomes to more people in a day. It requires a good understanding of the existing responsibilities of nurses and doctors, while also building backend systems capable of handling the modern influx of health data coming from more sources than ever before.

Designing better Electronic Health Records

            Case Study 1 chronicles the early history of Electronic Health Records, describing their naïve programming that was largely based on simple “If This -> Then That” logic (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016). This logic was codified as “rules” and were easily expandible; by simply adding additional rules to the program, it could alert practitioners to every conceivable piece of information about a patient. While a good framework to build software in bitesize steps, alert fatigue is hard to manage without a better way to determine the relative importance of each alert, complicated by the fact that different users will assign different values to those alerts – a physical therapist needs to know about broken bones sooner than a dentist, for example. In addition, these hard-coded rules do not share easily: if a database is organized differently, it will require its own custom version of those rules in order to function properly (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016).

            These problems originate from paper records, where information was assumed to be for local use only, and interoperability between large healthcare providers was not expected. Case study 7 goes over ten reasons data interoperability in healthcare is difficult but possibly not desired. Most importantly, healthcare data does not have a standardized format to this day, making every transfer between providers require manual data cleanup, and the healthcare industry’s need to be highly reliable while adhering to existing regulations has resulted in a slower rate of adopting new technology. Regulation also requires health data to remain private, which is easier to do if the data is not in an easily shareable format. Lastly, the incentives to create better EHRs have generally been moral and not financial. Only recent U.S. policy such as HITECH aligned moral and financial incentives to drive EHR adoption industry wide (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016).

            Today’s EHRs stand at the intersection of old paradigms and future ambitions. Companies like Epic and NextGen remain the premier vendors for EHR software, offering “walled gardens” of apps and features for their customers. This will eventually be a barrier to further progress – data is not currently stored in a standardized format, but as those standards are created and adopted, legacy systems that are hard coded to work with a different standard could slow the future “technodream” of seamless record transfers between providers and researchers.

            The future of health is heading in the direction of empowering the patient to have more control over how they interact with the healthcare industry. Companies like Apple now create consumer products that allow users to monitor their health in various ways without buying a bespoke medical device. Watches and armbands are made to track and monitor many health signs and give users greater visibility of their own state of wellness, making it easier to stay on track to achieve their medical goals. These personal devices also provide heaps of personal medical data, leading to a new vertical of apps and products offering services such as diet plans tailored specifically to the user, as case study 22 describes (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016). A challenge future iterations of EHRs need to solve is to integrate the largely unstructured data files these products output, and merge them with existing health records used by medical professionals. The promise of such innovation is greater access to healthcare by reducing costs, expanding the role of Telehealth services, and better monitoring of chronic conditions.

 

Introduction to the Data

           

            The focal point of this course has been a dataset from the Minnesota Department of Health, detailing macro-level financial data for 140 in-state hospitals for the year 2018. It covers topics about payments by various payers, and by various care units within the hospital. A second dataset contained admission information for many patients which was treated as a totally separate dataset. As it is only a single yearly report, there is no indication if 2018 was a noteworthy year in any way, and as such, predictive models were deemed a poor fit for this data.

Preparing the data for analysis required a handful of steps. As it was delivered as an Excel file, decorative features of the file had to be stripped. Next, null values had to be determined and standardized; the file contained multiple types of null values, and all were converted to be represented the same way (a blank cell) across all files. Further inspection of each file revealed many variables would be unfit for any analysis due to swaths of missing values. Columns were also renamed to a more consistent format. The final column names remain cumbersomely long; as no external metadata was provided, information about each column is only contained in its name. To possibly reduce runtimes and simplify queries, abbreviations may be advised. This can be done as part of an SQL SELECT statement (Microsoft, 2023), or using various functions in R such as ‘rename’ (Posit, 2023). Suggestions for abbreviations will appear in the attached data flow diagram. As the data was presented as multiple files, the only redundant columns were descriptive values of each hospital – name, location, ID, etc. – making it very close to being in a Third Normal Form, and not containing any duplicate information.

Reflecting on the cleaning process, it was important for me to consider real life workflows and overcoming my personal bias in software. As primarily an R user, I cleaned the data within R before exporting it back into Excel for dashboard construction. Those programs are not similar in how they treat text, so despite everything working in R, the resulting Excel file would not suit end-user needs without further tinkering due to how Excel treats blank cells in columns and values that resemble calendar dates. By failing to consider the needs of the end user (who requested an Excel dashboard), I created extra work for myself, which on a larger scale would have delayed my project. A product’s design can be split into four main phases: Planning, then three stages of design – Pre/Main/Post-design and development. Including users early in the planning phase helps guide the project towards a more usable state more likely to be adopted (Unertl, Holden, & Lorenzi, 2016). It would also “shape” the project sooner, reducing work that would be discarded later on, as I’ve done with this project.

Designing the Data Tables

 

The data given for this project was received in a relatively clean state; discussed earlier were the main steps to prepare it for analysis, and that really was all that was needed. “Third Normal Form” is a level of data normalization where each table has any fields that do not rely on the database’s primary key removed. This is to eliminate redundant data storage (Microsoft, 2023), and much of the data was already unique. The only data extracted from tables to create a “Main Table” was all descriptive fields for each hospital, such as Hospital Name, County, any hospital network affiliation, and their designation as a Critical Access Hospital. A table dedicated to bed counts was also created, which left four other tables largely derived from the original tables. As each table already had a “theme” to it, they were not rearranged into new tables.

Every table relates to each other exclusively through the “ID” key which identifies each hospital, and each hospital corresponds to a single entry in a table, giving each table a one-to-one relationship with each other. This makes joining tables with SQL trivial, as it’s the only key present in the database, and is consistently named across all tables. As currently designed, each table has the same length, as well as the same roster of hospitals, making the choice of SQL functions to join them straightforward. “FULL JOIN” adds all rows in the tables without checking for a match, while “INNER JOIN” would only add rows that have a match (a specific primary key value in both tables) (W3 Schools, 2023). With the current version of the database, both functions would result in similar tables, but in the future, should the database be updated with new hospitals, or with incomplete data, “INNER JOIN” will likely be more appropriate (Microsoft, 2023).

Manually entering individual column names may be clumsy, due to length. This is largely unavoidable, as we start trading ease of typing with code clarity, but there are some workarounds. First, each table has a strong theme to it; if you need department level data, importing all columns from the ‘CareUnitCharges’ table is likely sufficient. If only a few columns need to be excluded, they can be excluded using the DROP statement after importing the entire table within the SELECT statement.

In previous courses, I had a chance to join disparate data sources together, not just in SQL, but in R and Python. On my own computer, I’ve preferred to work entirely within R when I can, simply because I’m most familiar with it. However, R has some limitations with extraordinarily large datasets. R is a language that works entirely in the computer’s RAM, which puts a hard size and performance limit on any data imported into it. In this project, reading the hospital level data took less than a second, while the patient level data took about 8 seconds. What compounds this is my general workflow, in which I don’t usually unload the original tables after joining them, meaning I have duplicate data loaded into memory, which again I am limited by my work machine’s installed RAM. I have yet to find the limit to my work machine’s ability, but when I do and I run out of clever tricks, I’ll be glad to be familiar with SQL querying to create tables.

This course in particular highlighted many limitations with Microsoft’s Excel data analysis capabilities. This will be touched on more later, as Excel was used to create the dashboards, but Excel’s heritage of Office style automatic formatting proves woefully inadequate for data analysis and cleaning. Find & Replace is a clumsy tool to clean missing values, and Excel’s functions don’t natively account for missing values. Excel is also hard coded for coercing anything that looks like a date format into a date, resulting in 11-20 year olds in the patient dataset being given the age of “November 20”.

Dashboard Design

           

The Key Performance Indicators featured in the dashboard are motivated by the strategic framework laid out by the Balanced Scorecard. This project’s dataset featured mostly financial figures described in a few dimensions. Going with the patient-first mentality emphasized in the case studies, patient facing issues were the focus, and three of the four quadrants of the balanced scorecard served as inspiration for the KPIs present in the dashboard (Balanced Scorecard Institute, 2023).

The availability of medical beds and bassinets are used as a proxy for a hospital’s capacity to see patients, the idea being hospitals become a sort of temporary housing for inpatients, but the variability in inpatients’ length of stays have an impact on capacity to see a steady flow of outpatients visiting for a regular checkup. This sort of planning represents a tradeoff between providing adequate space to provide service, and expenses associated with running a larger facility. As outlined in Modelling for the planning and management of bed capacities in hospitals (Harper, 2002), hospital bed capacities require complex simulation modeling to accommodate for all the ways patients arrive to the hospital. Emergency admissions are random but require immediate attention, surgical patients have longer length of stays, but wait times for non-emergency visits also impact the bottom line of a hospital by way of throttling revenue from being unable to admit enough patients. The provided filters focus on regional views; some regions do not have local hospitals, so spatial views looking at groups of counties can identify regions where hospital access is too rural to be adequate service, especially in emergency situations. A map projection of Minnesota is also provided to visualize potential county groupings to study bed availability. This dashboard is valuable in the context of the Internal Process quadrant of the Balanced Scorecard, as bed management is one of the key logistical features of a hospital – a potential bottleneck to revenue, a frustration to patients, and a big feature in the design of the physical layout of the hospital (Balanced Scorecard Institute, 2023).

Data about self-paying patients serves as a proxy for underserved patients. The U.S. healthcare system has a heavy reliance on insurance to pay for medical procedures, so self-payers represent individuals without health insurance, or without enough health insurance. Similar filters are provided here, to break down geographic patterns in self-paying patients, as well as comparing various healthcare systems against each other. This dashboard is motivated by the Customer aspect of the Balanced Scorecard, but its view is more from the dual perspective of healthcare providers and insurance companies (Balanced Scorecard Institute, 2023). More detailed data could break down payer information by type of service, to see what types of care are more likely to be paid out of pocket and adapting those services to reduce financial burden and increase the likelihood of insurance actually covering the service.

Patient’s Length of Stay is broken down by Age Group, and can be filtered by hospital department and nature of the admission. Primarily, this dashboard concerns the Customer quadrant of the Balanced Scorecard, as shorter stays reduce healthcare costs and likely increase customer satisfaction. This is tensioned by the revenue generated by an inpatient versus an outpatient; Hospital Revenue, Cost, and Contribution Margin in Inpatient Versus Outpatient Primary Total Joint Arthroplasty (Christensen, et al., 2022) examined the contribution margin (difference between patient revenue and direct costs of care) generated by inpatients and outpatients, and found inpatients generated substantially more profit for the hospital than an outpatient. This means average Lengths of Stay impacts the hospital’s bottom line, but a patient-first mentality as constantly advocated for in many of the case studies found in Healthcare Information Management Systems (Weaver, Ball, Kim, & Kiel, 2016) means we ought to reduce Length of Stays to provide a higher quality of care, at the cost of profit maximization. The Visitors dashboard is related to the Length of Stays and Beds dashboard, as it can impact the physical design of the hospital room. The bed and medical equipment are obvious inclusions in a floor plan, but a hospital wing that sees many family members staying for extended periods or even overnight requires additional furniture to accommodate them.

Charges to the patient are broken down by department in the “PatientChargesDept” dashboard. This relates to the financial quadrant of the Balanced Scorecard, as it serves as an overview of the services each healthcare affiliation provides (Balanced Scorecard Institute, 2023). Different clinics and hospitals will have different specialties – some may employ many surgeons, while others specialize in mental health – and knowing what departments generate the majority of patient revenue helps allocate funding and potential expansions to hospital space and services. Future studies can compare revenue against direct costs and number of patient admissions to analyze the most cost-effective wings of the hospital and identify types of care that are less common, more expensive, but may be offset by profits from other aspects of the business.

Conclusion

           

The future of healthcare is patient centric. It has not been financially or technologically possible to be so in the past, but modern legislation has better aligned industry incentives to modernize their underlying technology, and the proliferation of modern computing has led to innovative ways to collect real time data from users and integrate that data into new insights for practitioners to better serve their patients. The future promises better access to healthcare via lower costs, more personalized care plans, and expanded use of telehealth services. Healthcare still lags in adopting the newest technology into its regular workflow, as well as establishing standardized data practices to improve data compatibility across various Electronic Health Records, but the expertise is there, and modern events such as the Covid-19 Pandemic underline the importance of improving access, as well as expanding how patients can interact with their healthcare professionals.
