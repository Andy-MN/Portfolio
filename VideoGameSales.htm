Abstract

            Publishers need to spend company funds on profit maximizing products, which has become more important as video game sales have increased, along with the associated costs of producing games. Linear regression is conducted to find the effects of genre, publisher, critical reception, and other categorical considerations on Global Sales. The final model weighs the value of a strong marketing campaign heavily, with User Scores being insignificant despite finding professional critic’s opinions significant enough to include. Future studies should experiment with further data stratification, as well as distinguishing the effects of marketing with the effects of being a “safe bet” game part of a larger franchise.

Introduction

            Video games are big business, growing in revenue year over year for decades (Wallach, 2020). More types of games are regularly published, as the biggest firms diversify their offerings, while tools for amateur creators mature over time, all leading to more games being released in a year than ever before. At the same time, costs associated with developing games has ballooned over time. The computer hardware gets more sophisticated, more capable of detail, more capable of handling more complex computations, demanding artists and programmers to take more time to create than it did just a few years ago (Koster, 2018). As games get more expensive to make, they either need to become more expensive to the consumer, or need to sell in greater numbers, to break even financially.

            This gives rise to the need to discriminate what kinds of games to produce and ultimately sell. Games are categorized by “genres” that describe the general gameplay experience of the product, as well as the “platform” it is available for sale on. Games may have multiple genres and may be available for many platforms. In addition, video games have a reasonably mature industry of writers who create product reviews for both video game platforms, as well as individual video games, and such media coverage can generate sales where marketing cannot.

            To that end, the objective of this study is to evaluate the effect of marketing, platform availability, genre, critical review, and how that can possibly affect the global sales of a video game. Statistical modeling should provide some numerical insights to reinforce preconceived notions of what we see customers as valuing the most.

Methods

            Data was provided by the instructor of the course, and features data from Metacritic, including aggregate critic scores, aggregate user scores, and the number of critics/users that the aggregate includes. Both are measured on a base 10 scale – critics range from 0 to 100, while users range from 0 to 10 with a single decimal point of precision. Because not every media outlet uses the same “rating system”, Metacritic converts into their 0-100 scale. For example, if a writer gives a game 4 out of 5 stars, Metacritic would convert that into a score of 80 out of 100. Multiple new variables were created for the purpose of experimentation, such as converting the 0-100 scale back to a 5-star rating, as well as a variable called “80plus” that takes on a binary value depending on if the critic score is greater than or equal to 80. Historically, some game makers have received a monetary bonus if their game received a sufficiently high Metacritic rating (Yin-Poole, 2012), so a threshold of 80 was subjectively chosen based on being slightly lower than historical bonus thresholds.

Sales data is split into 3 different regions – Japan, Europe, and North America – and summed up as “Global Sales”, our dependent variable. Region specific sales will be evaluated separately to see how the generic “Global Sales” model explains the sales patterns in each region – a brief expansion There is also a “Marketing” variable to express the amount of marketing focus the game received, taking on a value of 0, 1, or 2. Due to the range of values in the sales data, multiple transformations were considered, but a logarithmic scale was settled on to better fit linear regression modeling.

Notably, there is no data about actual marketing budgets or general development costs in the dataset, as well as no record of the original price of the games. Both would greatly influence the amount of units required to profit from a given game. In addition, each video game platform is not equally popular, and a game console that sells twice the units is more likely to see sales of its associated games to be naturally greater than games available on less popular platforms. The only information on console sales in the data is derived from sales of “tie in” games: Wii Sports, the best-selling game in the dataset, was bundled for free with every new Nintendo Wii, so its sales are an approximation of total Wii sales. The various platforms in the dataset were additionally generalized to be associated with their respective manufacturers: Nintendo, Sony Entertainment, or Microsoft. In other words, every game for Wii, WiiU, Game Boy Advance, etc., will all have a dummy variable tag them as being on a “Nintendo” platform.

Ordinary Linear Regression will be the statistical test to evaluate the data’s ability to explain the variation in Global Sales. Criteria for selecting the final model was a combination of Rsquare value, AICc, and general model complexity. A variable such as “Developer” is categorical with many distinct values, and each would have a different coefficient associated with it, and very likely overfit the data. Individual effects were measured for each variable, as well as combined to fit multiple models for estimating Global Sales, and those models were also evaluated for effectiveness in explaining the three primary regions. All data transformations were done in R code before models and visualizations were generated with SAS Studio scripts.

Results

            Among the single effect models, Marketing proved to be the most powerful effect to include in the regression models. All four models discussed in this section include it, as the model Global Sales = B1*Marketing + C resulted in an R2 of 0.53. The vast majority of games in the dataset have a value of Marketing = 0, indicating no marketing focus. Only 77 games have a Marketing Priority of 2.

            The first model fit was the “all inclusive” model (Model 1), with every independent variable included in the regression to serve as a baseline expectation. With an adjusted R2 value of 0.69, it fits to the data reasonably well, but standard errors for many of the parameters render much of the estimates not meaningfully different from random chance. It also includes an overwhelming number of dummy variables that are removed from all the following models.

            Model 2 is derived by removing statistically insignificant parameters from Model 1, resulting in 6 estimators: Marketing, the three main console manufacturers (Nintendo, Sony, Microsoft), Critic Scores, and Number of Critics who contributed to the score. Adjusted R2 fell to 0.63 while model complexity fell dramatically. The effect size of each independent variable is reasonably sized as well – this model originally included User Scores, but while the parameter was found to be statistically insignificant, the effect size was miniscule. The Sony dummy variable had a slightly larger effect size than Nintendo or Microsoft, but the strongest effect remained the Marketing variable. Model 2 was ultimately selected as the “final” model, but two more models will be commented here.

Model 3 is a variation of Model 2, replacing the generalized “platform” variables with the actual names of the consoles the game was sold for. R2 values improved slightly here, but not all parameters were found to be statistically different from random chance. AICc criteria also found this model to be worse than Model 2, alongside the model’s inherent complexity making it harder to understand.

When testing region specific models, Model 2 mapped well onto North America and Europe data well, with nearly identical results to the general model, but only yielded an R2 value of 0.3 for Japan sales. Further experimentation was conducted to find a better model for Japan, which is labeled as Model 4 in the appendix.

Discussion

            Marketing being the best indicator of a best seller is no real surprise, and possibly misleading. Careful examination of the games in the dataset with the highest Global Sales, most of them are either part of a long running franchise with an established customer base, or games for the Nintendo Wii which had a substantial sales record each year it was sold, again yielding a large established customer base. Only a handful of the top 100 games in the database were the first entries of a new IP, such as Gears of War, Driver, or Just Dance. Most of the top 100 have a Marketing Priority of 2, and the lowest selling game with Marketing = 2 is Battlefield One at #198 in the Global Sales ranking. The concern here has to do with self-fulfilling prophecies: as these games have proven to sell in the past, it is easier to convince stakeholders to provide a substantial marketing budget on these games they likely view as “safe bets”, rather than push new, unproven projects. With expanded marketing, more customers are aware of the product, and thus greater sales are achieved on average. Further studies into this data should focus on separating the effects of the marketing campaigns with the effects of being part of a larger franchise, which would require significant effort and care.

            The data also does not handle “multiplatform” games appropriately enough, but given the timespan the dataset draws on, it is likely difficult to fix. Most of the data is between the years 2000 and 2016, and especially in the era where the market consisted of the Nintendo’s Wii, Sony’s PlayStation 3, and Microsoft’s Xbox 360, the Wii would often receive entirely distinct versions of a video game bearing the same name as a game sold on PlayStation or Xbox, due to the Wii’s underperformance in hardware quality. So, a game such as Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 3 would be developed by a different company for the Wii version than the other versions, and often be substantially lower in quality as a result. The dataset simply treats each different version of the game as completely distinct, but it may be appropriate in a future study to merge sufficiently similar game versions (such as Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3 versions).

            The final model finds some value in Critic Scores as a predictor for sales. This informs some strategy for the publisher: a game expected to receive lower scores from critics should be withheld from previews, instead of providing media preview copies ahead of the official street date, in order to not undercut any marketing spent on the game. Critic Count is possibly a proxy for marketing, as games media companies probably find their readers are more likely to click on an article about games they’re personally excited about rather than a game they’ve never heard of. Therefore, if a game has many critics reviewing it, it’s likely an indication the game is considered a “major” release no advertisement supported media company can ignore – the same companies that will run the marketing campaigns of the big games.

            The “Genre” variable may have some value to examine in future studies; each of the three major game publishers have different tendencies to publish certain genres instead of others – a graph in the appendix explains the differences sufficiently. The main challenge here is the bias inherent in the variable. Much of modern gaming is comprised of games covering multiple genres of play. A “Shooter” may have elements of an “RPG” and/or “Puzzle” game, but only be labeled as the first. “Action” games cover a massive space of games, serving as a sort of catch-all for any game that has both a button to punch and a button to jump. Nonetheless, stratifying the data by genre may indicate marketing’s varying effects on sales.

            The dataset on a whole does not explain Japan sales patterns well. Metacritic is primarily aimed at English speaking publications, so opinions by local media in Japan is not factored into the Critic Scores. In addition, not every game makes it to Japan, or leave Japan; much of the data was recoded as “missing” because it had zero sales in Japan due to no local release. In Model 4, Microsoft publishing a game was a huge influence on the sales total for Japan. Microsoft has historically had a very tough time selling Xboxes in Japan (Corden, 2021), so any game made for it will naturally sell poorly in Japan, hence the large effect size.
