Balanced Scorecard Analysis for Asmodee Group

 

Andy Williams

           

 

            Asmodee is a multimedia company, primarily focused on tabletop board games, that operates across the globe. Originally founded in France by Marc Nunes, it has expanded in size and scope, and has gone through multiple acquisitions by larger firms, most recently the Embracer Group, who completed their acquisition earlier this year. The goal of Asmodee has always been to manage the business side of the naturally creative process of making board games, and today that means Asmodee manages over 20 in-house studios who can focus on product development. They have also grown to such a size that they benefit greatly from a large back catalog of IPs, as well as partnering with other brands, such as Disney owned Star Wars and Marvel, and publishing a wide range of games using those IPs. With their combination of a strong business model, wide pool of talent for game creation, and a global market presence, they’ve positioned themselves as one of the major players in the hobby toy industry (Embracer Group, 2021). This paper evaluates Asmodee on the four quadrants of the Balanced Scorecard as developed by the Balanced Scorecard Institute: Financial Performance, Internal Processes, Customer Experience, and Learning & Growth.

            Financially, Asmodee is very healthy. Their business has routinely been profitable, thanks to a combination of long term best sellers such as Catan and Ticket to Ride, and regular publishing of new games, about 50 per year (Embracer Group, 2021). Their business is very diversified and vertically integrated: they own their own properties, creative studios, and distribution network. The only things they outsource is retail presence and physical manufacturing. They operate in every part of the work, and publish games in many languages, often simultaneously. The net effect is every new product is very low risk: low R&D costs and good product margins lead to early profits on most new products they create (Embracer Group, 2021).

            Asmodee’s internal process is largely based on their growth strategy. Since their inception, they acquire successful studios and IPs and expand those IPs into new markets. As they grew their portfolio, they began to cross-sell IPs across different markets in the hobby game industry. For example, they have published many games with Disney’s Star Wars license, first with original designs such as “X-Wing Miniatures Game” and “Star Wars Destiny Card Game”, and later with combining Star Wars with their other established brands, such as the “Pandemic Game System”, and the “Unlock!” and “Love Letter” game lines (Embracer Group, 2021). Even during the Covid-19 crisis, they have managed to keep physical manufacturing steady to provide new and existing products for all their major brands, rarely being out of stock for long. This strategy has led to many successful product lines, while also minimizing risks associated with new and innovative games. They also manage distribution for companies outside their corporate umbrella, such as CMON Games (CMON, 2019).

            Culturally, Asmodee is largely defined by their in-house studios. Asmodee themselves is largely handling the business side of the hobby games market, leaving their studios to focus on developing and designing new products. This leaves every studio with the ability to cultivate their own creative culture, which is hard to find information about outside of major press releases. For example, Corey Konieczka was a longtime designer at Fantasy Flight Games Studio, but recently left to form a new studio, Unexpected Games, while remaining within Asmodee (Langsworthy, 2019). It’s hard to know if this kind of shift is a result of cultural changes within Fantasy Flight, or a restructuring led by Asmodee that reduced the scope of Fantasy Flight’s creative work that forced Konieczka to exit the studio. Nonetheless, Asmodee is proud to allow their studios to work creatively and mostly independently (Embracer Group, 2021). This leaves their learning & growth of their workforce up to the individual studios – Asmodee largely just writes the pay checks and maintains IP rights.

            For the customers of Asmodee’s games, there are pains that go along their relative immense size. Because Asmodee is such a large company with many SKUs, they have discontinued their parts replacement service branch (Asmodee North America, 2020). The norm up to that point across the entire board game industry is to mail replacement pieces to individuals who fill out an online form. Their new method is to use their existing distribution network to provide entire replacement copies of the product, which could lead to massive waste if managed incorrectly. It also makes it harder to find replacements for games bought via secondhand sales. The upside to the customer, however, is largely positive. Asmodee can produce a nearly infinite range of products for any of their IPs, and combine them in many ways to make new games. For example, “Pandemic” began as a game about curing global diseases, but the brand has expanded into developing games with the Star Wars and World of Warcraft licenses, bringing value to both Pandemic fans and Star Wars fans. They can also better support games long term, as they do not have the financial risks a small independent studio may face (Embracer Group, 2021). Their games are also more readily available in multiple languages, thanks to inhouse localization teams.

            In summary, Asmodee rates relatively strong in a Balanced Scorecard analysis. The most readily available information concerns their finances and customer sentiment, which both rate strongly. Their business outlook is strong, and they’ve created many long term customers thanks to their vast array of high value IPs and creative studios, as well as brand partnerships with Disney and others. They offer stability and profitability in a market defined by small businesses with tight budgets who need to outsource most tasks to bring a game to market. Their lack of direct marketing and decentralized leadership structure makes it hard to evaluate internal workings of the company. Their business outlook is bright, with much room to grow exponentially.
