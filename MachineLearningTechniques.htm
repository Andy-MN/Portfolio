Introduction

            In the United States, healthcare providers and insurance companies work in parallel to operate and fund hospitals, pharmacies, and clinics. Hospitals provide services, and most of their patients pay for those services through their insurance plan. In 2020, 91% of Americans were covered by health insurance for at least part of the year (Keisler-Starkey & Bunch, 2021). With patients rarely paying out of pocket, hospitals end up negotiating business-to-business with the insurance companies, who rely on paperwork to understand what services were provided and what that means for payment. This provides cover for committing fraud; billing for services that were never performed (which a patient could easily identify and dispute), altering the invoice to charge for more expensive services, adding medically unnecessary services, and misbranding prescription drugs are all ways that fraud could be committed, and has been committed (United States Government Accountability Office, 2016).

            The effects of fraud are different for each party. For the insurance company, it is likely to greatly affect the fees they must charge to their customers. Some fraud is always assumed but minimizing it as much as they can allows them to reduce insurance premiums to more accurately reflect the costs and risks of their insurance business in their fees, and thus better serve customers. Legitimate and illegitimate insurance claims are paid out of the same checkbook, so when fraudulent behavior rises above “background noise” levels, it means legitimate customers are stuck paying for it in the long run. Fraud also redirects money away from “good” causes, requiring either more money to be pumped into the insurance program (or relief programs if we extend our view to natural disaster and government led initiatives) to get the intended effect, or increased scrutiny on all claims, which can lead to fewer types of medical services covered by insurance. With the recent Covid-19 Pandemic, the U.S. Government acted as a sort of insurance entity to temporarily cover some business costs for small businesses, but due to a lack of oversight, much of the money allocated to the program went to grifters taking advantage of the bad situation (Dilanian & Strickler, 2022). The scope of the PPP program is much beyond what a private insurance company is capable of doing (or wanting to do), but the problem is the same: fraud balloons the cost to help people.

            As service providers, hospitals and clinics are affected by fraud in different ways than insurance providers. They must adhere to government regulations, maintain appropriate stock levels of prescription drugs, efficiently schedule patients for surgery and preventative care, and many, many other services. Fraud erodes their ability to do these things; drugs go missing, paperwork gets filed improperly to cover the fraud, hospital beds get underutilized, but most importantly, community trust erodes. For a nonprofit healthcare system like CentraCare, a loss in trust ends up affecting the broader community. CentraCare operates 9 hospitals, 14 senior service locations, and many clinics (CentraCare, 2023). If CentraCare’s reputation takes a hit, it may lead to more people forgoing medical services and seeking more controversial “alternative medicines”. It could also affect the bottom line via malpractice lawsuits, which a clinic that worked with CentraCare Systems for decades got hit with in 2017 (Walsh, 2022).

So as it is in the insurance companies interest to minimize fraud in order to reduce healthcare costs, hospital systems like CentraCare wish to minimize fraud to maximize the benefits they can provide to their communities. There is likely some merit to reduce fraud for the benefit of healthcare professionals themselves, to help them maintain a healthy and productive work environment, but this notion remains speculative for now. Covid-19 escalated the challenge   nurses and doctors face, adding very possible death by deadly disease to their list of problems (Lucchesi, 2021). With their mention, it is clear fraud can affect everyone in the healthcare sector – patients, professionals, and stakeholders.

In this paper, machine learning techniques are evaluated for their ability to detect fraudulent healthcare providers. While all real-world cases should be scrutinized by real world humans, computer algorithms can help filter down the cases to the ones that are most likely to be fraudulent and allow more time to investigate those claims. This is the goal. Fraud, by its nature, is constantly evolving with changes in government policy, emergent pandemics, and broader economic trends. Any algorithm meant to flag fraud will inevitably have an expiration date, so the focus of this paper is less about finding particular features of fraudulent behavior and instead interrogating the specific methods we can use to find possible fraud in the first place.

Methodology

            The Healthcare Fraud dataset comes from the public machine learning website Kaggle (Kaggle.com) and contains claim level information. Each row is one claim from one healthcare beneficiary, but multiple claims can be associated with the same beneficiary and/or provider. Because this project focuses on the activity of providers, who are much more likely to be involved with fraudulent behavior than beneficiaries (United States Government Accountability Office, 2016), the claim and beneficiary data was aggregated to provide summary statistics for each provider. The data labels each provider as being either “Potentially Fraudulent” or not; it does not distinguish which claims or patients a provider serviced was found fraudulent. This lack of nuance hurts the actionable parts of the analysis as well: it is not reasonable to assume that a provider will always commit fraud, but rather it is likely they will only engage in fraudulent behavior in specific circumstances in order to minimize the risks associated with doing crime (Such as “doing time”).

While the data as provided was clean, the metadata was not complete. The data had been anonymized so much that it was difficult to know what regions of the United States it represented and did not reference a source external to Kaggle. Inspection of the data did not reveal any features that may indicate the data is fake, but without proper sourcing, this project assumes, at a minimum, the data is “arbitrary”, suitable for testing methods, but not suitable to take action after the final analysis. This project therefore focuses more on the methods rather than specific details of the models generated. Fraud changes over time, when laws and practices change, and this dataset is quite outdated. Claim dates are centered around the year 2009, before the Affordable Care Act was signed into law and transformed much of U.S. healthcare (U.S. HHS, 2023). The dataset also uses ICD-9 standards for diagnosis codes which have been replaced with ICD-10 in the years since this data was collected (HIPAA Space, 2023).

The primary modeling techniques interrogated in this project were Decision Trees, Random Forests (which is an extension of decision tree modeling), and Logistic Regression. All models tested to predict which of the two groups (fraudsters and law-abiders) a provider was associated with. The complete list of rows will be in the appendix, but generally, the claim level was aggregated to find the number of unique inpatients and outpatients each provider saw, how many diagnosis codes they were likely to list (GAO described “upcoding” as a common scheme, as well as over-diagnosing, but upcoding investigation requires domain specific knowledge the author does not possess), reimbursement amounts, and lengths of hospital stays (for inpatients only). The labeled data was split into training and testing portions, 70% and 30% respectively.

All data cleaning, transforming, modeling, and visualization was generated using the R programming language (https://www.r-project.org/). R is an open-source programming language with built-in statistical functions, but this project relies more heavily on function packages developed by Hadley Wickham, lead data scientist at Posit (https://www.tidyverse.org/). Specific packages are listed within the R script, as well as summarized in the appendix and their versions. Coding was performed in RStudio.

Results

Figure 1: Number of Fraudulent Providers

            The Healthcare Fraud dataset labels 91% of providers as nonfraudulent (represented with a label of ‘0’). This is good for the reputation of healthcare providers everywhere but poses a problem for classification models. Some models were therefore trained on a version of the training split that oversamples the fraudulent providers to create a 1:1 ratio in the groups to understand how that affected prediction.

Figure 2: Number of Inpatient claims submitted by Fraudulent/Nonfraudulent Providers

            For a long time, inpatient data was evaluated to predict what fraudulent claims looked like, rather than looking at providers specifically. This proved difficult and was soon abandoned, but comparing how many claims are associated with fraudulent providers and the percentage of providers overall, it was apparent fraudulent providers see a substantial volume of patients and file many claims. Not all providers saw both inpatients and outpatients, but of the 2092 providers that did, 440 were labeled as fraudulent (21%).

            Three decision trees were created on three versions of the data: the full dataset (for exploratory purposes), the training sample, and the oversampled version of the training split. The oversampled version of the data yielded the best predictive power. The challenge of there being a huge imbalance in the sizes of the two groups of providers was decision trees were better at finding “hay” in the “haystack” than it was at finding the “needles” (the fraudulent providers). This can still suit our needs, however: as fraud is ultimately investigated by humans, being able to filter out claims and providers not likely to be fraudulent significantly reduces the workload of an investigator. With the oversampled dataset, a balance is struck between correctly assigning providers to the two groups and reducing instances of false positives.

Figure 3&4: Oversampled Data Confusion Matrix and evaluation metrics

            Some discussion on the Decision Tree’s metrics are needed. The model was trained on the oversampled version of the training split, but evaluated on the original and unbalanced testing split. The “No Information Rate” decimal represents the largest group in the test data – 90% of the data is labeled as nonfraudulent. Since the accuracy of the model is only 80%, this results in the worst p-value possible. This suggests that this model specifically does not give us enough information to determine fraudulent providers, despite its high accuracy.

Figure 5: Decision Tree For Oversampled Data

            Random Forests are an extension of Decision Trees meant to reduce chance of bias and overfitting the data, common problems with Decision Trees (IBM, 2023). Implementing them in R code is very similar to Decision Trees but a single tree visualization cannot be generated, so only evaluation metrics are provided. The oversampled dataset was also used here.

Figure 6: Random Forest metrics on Oversampled Data

            The Random Forest metrics paint a much better picture. The same data was fed into the Random Forest model as was the Decision Tree (balanced training, unbalanced testing data). A p-value well below common thresholds was found, so the rest of the metrics are worth evaluating. Because of the unbalanced testing data, it is not a surprise to see the Sensitivity statistic fall below the Specificity metric: just like Decision Trees, a Random Forest is going to be better at predicting the largest group present in the data, which would be the nonfraudulent providers (0’s in the data). Finding nonfraudulent providers is almost as good as finding fraudulent ones for our purposes, as we can use the model as a filter instead of letting it make choices instead of a human.

            Logistic Regression was used to probabilistically determine which group each provider belonged to. The oversampled data trained the model, and that was used to predict the unbalanced test split. The first model ran included all features the Decision Tree models included before pruning them away if their respective p-values were above 0.05. After running various combinations of variables, a model including Number of Inpatients, Number of Outpatients, and Average Reimbursement for Inpatient claims was settled on. A confusion matrix and evaluation metrics output is provided below.

Figure 7: Logistic Regression fit with 3 predictors

            The Logistic Regression model achieves similar scores in accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity as the Decision Tree models. Consistent with other models, this one is very good at sorting out nonfraudulent providers but leaves many fraudulent providers undetected. An ROC curve plot is provided in the Appendix.

           

 

Discussion

            Regardless of the particular algorithm used, a similar pattern of predictive power was seen. Decision Trees and Logistic Regression produce different outputs, but the features they selected as having the most predictive power were somewhat similar. Decision Trees effectively “rank” the variables, choosing the best splits first before creating new branches with the other variables. Logistic Regression outputs a model that assigns coefficients to each variable describing how the “odds” change as each variable change. Both suffer from similar issues in the dataset: due to fraudulent providers making up such a low percentage of total providers, there is a high Specificity metric for each model but a relatively low Sensitivity metric. Because we are sorting providers into only 2 large groups without additional nuance, these models have value in filtering out providers “most likely to not be fraudulent”. Oversampling or undersampling the training data in order to better balance the two groups alleviates this issue somewhat, but the accuracy improvements in predicting fresh test data is only marginal. Numeric data proved difficult to use in some cases. For example, deductible amounts in this dataset went virtually unused, due to lack of variance, but also due to a lack of normality. The nature of how deductibles are standardized in healthcare plans likely make them poor predictors, but recoding them as some sort of factor may have some merit (deductible/no deductible, for example).

            Future analysis can focus on mixed strategies, using multiple algorithms and checking where they “agree”.  Models discussed here only matched in what group they assigned a provider to only about 50% of the time, showing one model may be picking up on details the other is not. In this case, when the Random Forest and Logistic Regression models agreed, it perfectly assigned all nonfraudulent providers into the nonfraud group. When sorting fraudulent providers, the models most often disagreed with each other, and that disagreement could be used as an indicator of fraudulent provider behavior.

            There were some unused details in the analysis, omitted due to lack of domain knowledge. As schemes like “upcoding” is common in healthcare fraud (United States Government Accountability Office, 2016), correlations of diagnosis and procedure codes could be used to create fraud models. This would require some way of putting codes into a hierarchy based on cost of each procedure, what costs patients are responsible for and what insurance pays for. Up to date data is a must, with modern codes, and models should be reran as government and healthcare policies change. Fraud reacts to the practices of the time, so while a model like a Decision Tree may remain relevant and valuable, the weights it assigns to different bits of data must change with time. This all points to rigorous data collection practices and working with domain experts to keep metadata about medical best practices up to date.
